There are two failure modes for content briefs. The thin brief gives a keyword and a word count and produces a draft that needs rewriting. The exhaustive brief runs six pages, gets skimmed, and produces the same draft. The difference between them is not length, it is whether the document carries decisions.

## Brief the cluster, not the keyword

The first structural fix is to stop briefing individual keywords. Hand the writer the group of keywords a single page should satisfy, along with the page type it should be. Briefing keywords one at a time is the most common route to two pages on the same site competing for the same intent. The [keyword clustering method](/tools/keyword-cluster-mapper) covers how to build those groups.

## Lead with the decisions

A brief is useful in proportion to how many hard choices it settles. In rough order of impact:

1. **Intent, in one sentence.** What is the reader trying to do? A writer who knows the job will structure the page better than one following a heading list.
2. **The angle.** What will this page contain that the current top ten does not? Without this the writer summarises page one, and a summary of page one has no reason to outrank page one.
3. **The format the SERP rewards.** If the results are all comparison tables, say so. The format is usually not negotiable, and discovering that after the draft is expensive.
4. **Internal links, in both directions.** Which pages this links to, and which should link back. Left to the writer, this becomes an afterthought and the cluster never connects.
5. **FAQ questions, in exact-match phrasing.** Pull them from People Also Ask so the wording matches how people actually ask.

Word count belongs near the bottom. It is the field most briefs lead with and the one least likely to change quality, because a target copied from competitors encodes their padding as well as their substance.

## The template

`Cluster | Primary keyword | Supporting keywords | Page type | Intent (one sentence) | Angle | SERP format | Links out | Links in | FAQ questions | Word target`

Eleven fields, one page. If a field is not going to change what the writer produces, cut it. Reference material can live somewhere the writer can reach rather than in the middle of the instructions.

## The angle is the field that matters most

If you only improve one part of your briefs, improve this one. Most briefs implicitly ask for a well-written version of what already ranks, and a well-written version of what already ranks does not displace it. The angle can be original data, a genuinely different framing, practitioner detail the ranking pages lack, or a scope decision such as covering the case everyone else skips.

Stating it in the brief forces the question to be answered before writing starts, when it is still cheap to answer.

# How to compare scheduling software with payment processing

**Scheduling software with payment processing** is booking software that lets clients reserve a time slot and pay for it in the same step, usually by connecting a processor like Stripe, PayPal, or Square to the booking page. Comparing these tools well means checking four things: which processors each one supports, how it stops two people from booking the same slot, what a team actually pays per user, and what the payment processor itself charges on top of the subscription.

Most buying guides for this category stop at a feature checklist. That's not enough once you have more than one person taking bookings, because the real risk isn't "does it take payments," it's "does it take payments *and* keep a five-person calendar from double-booking a client while doing it." This guide walks through both questions side by side, plus a worked example you can adapt to your own team.

## What "scheduling software with payment processing" actually means

At its simplest, this category combines two things that used to be separate: an online booking calendar and a payment gateway. Instead of sending a client a calendar link and then a separate invoice, the booking page collects a card (or PayPal balance) at the moment the appointment is confirmed. That matters for service businesses because a paid booking is a lot harder to no-show on than a free one.

The processor doing the actual charging is almost always a third party, Stripe, PayPal, or Square, plugged into the scheduling tool rather than built from scratch by the scheduling vendor. That's worth knowing because it changes where the fees come from: the scheduling platform may charge a subscription, and the processor separately charges a per-transaction fee. You'll want both numbers before you commit.

## The four criteria that actually decide this comparison

Every platform in this category will tell you it "takes payments." The differences that matter for a small team show up in these four areas:

- **Processor support**: does it connect to the processor your business already uses, and does that connection work on the free or entry plan, or only on a paid tier?

- **Double-booking prevention**: when two staff members share a calendar, or one person is booked across two connected calendars, does the tool block the conflict automatically?

- **Per-user pricing at team size**: a tool that looks cheap for one person can cost meaningfully more once you add three or five teammates.

- **Fees on top of the subscription**: the processor's transaction fee is separate from the software's subscription fee, and it usually isn't waived just because you're paying for a premium plan.

Cal.com's homepage states plainly that it handles calendar cross-referencing so users "don't have to worry about double bookings," and its collective event type is built to check every participant's schedule automatically when a booking includes more than one host. That's a specific, built-in behavior, not just a marketing claim about calendar sync.

Acuity's own site says its scheduling "prevent[s] double-bookings with calendar sync" as part of automating a business's day-to-day booking flow. That's the extent of what Acuity's public pages confirm about the mechanism; they don't detail how conflicts are resolved across multiple staff members sharing one client-facing page.

For Setmore and Square Appointments, the material available from each vendor's own site focuses on the payment side of the experience rather than describing calendar conflict handling in the same explicit terms Cal.com and Acuity use. That doesn't mean the capability isn't there. It means you should verify it yourself before rolling out a multi-person calendar: create two staff profiles, book the same time slot for one of them from two different browser sessions, and confirm the second attempt is blocked rather than silently accepted. That five-minute test tells you more than any feature list.

## What payment processing fees apply beyond the subscription?

This is the number most comparisons skip, and it's the one that compounds fastest as booking volume grows. Setmore is the most transparent source available here: it states plainly that "there are zero extra charges beyond the transaction fees set by our payment partners," and then lists those partner rates directly. **According to**[**Setmore's own fee table**](https://www.setmore.com/features/payments/us)**, Square charges 2.6% + $0.15 for in-person transactions and 2.9% + $0.30 online, Stripe charges 2.9% + $0.30, PayPal charges 3.49% + $0.49, and LawPay charges 2.99% + $0.30.** Setmore notes rates may vary by country.

Cal.com's and Acuity's own pages describe the Stripe/PayPal/Square connections functionally but don't publish a comparable per-transaction rate table in the material available, so don't assume their processor fees match Setmore's; check the processor's own current rate card for the account type you'll use. Square publishes its rates on a separate [fees page](https://squareup.com/us/en/payments/our-fees), which is the right place to check current numbers for any Square-based setup rather than relying on a third party's summary.

## A worked example: comparing three tools for a five-person team

Here's a hypothetical scenario to make the criteria concrete. Say a five-person consulting team wants to accept Stripe payments for paid discovery calls, needs shared availability so any of the five can be booked, and wants to know the real monthly cost before signing up.

- **Check processor fit first.** If the team already invoices through Stripe, confirm the scheduling tool's Stripe connection is available on the plan tier they're actually considering, not just mentioned somewhere in the marketing copy.

- **Price the team, not the individual.** A per-user monthly rate multiplied by five looks very different from a flat team price. Using Cal.com's published Teams rate of $12 per user per month billed yearly as an example, five users would total $60/month before any add-ons, versus checking whether a competitor's team tier bundles seats differently.

- **Run the double-booking test.** Before committing, book overlapping slots for the same team member from two devices and confirm the platform blocks the second one, rather than trusting a feature list.

- **Add the processor's cut.** Take the average paid-call value and multiply by the processor's published rate (for example, Stripe's typical online rate, which Setmore lists as 2.9% + $0.30 in its own fee table) to see what payment processing actually costs per booking, separate from the software subscription.

That four-step exercise, repeated for each platform on your shortlist, turns a features page into an actual monthly cost and a confirmed behavior, which is a more useful comparison than a checkmark grid.

## What is the best booking and payment system for a small business?

There's no single answer that holds for every business, because the right fit depends on which processor you already use, how many people share the calendar, and whether industry-specific compliance (healthcare, legal) is a requirement. A solo practitioner who just needs Stripe or PayPal on a free plan has a very different shortlist than a five-person team that needs shared availability and round-robin assignment.

What you can say with confidence: check processor support against your existing payment relationships first, since switching processors later is more disruptive than switching booking pages. Then test double-booking behavior yourself with the overlapping-slot method described above, rather than trusting a features page. Then price the team size you actually have, using each vendor's current published rate rather than a per-seat estimate. Finally, add the processor's own transaction fee, which Setmore's rate table shows can range from roughly 2.6% to 3.49% plus a small flat fee depending on the processor, on top of whatever the software itself charges.

## Putting the comparison to work

Confirm processor support against the plan tier you'll actually pay for, test double-booking prevention with a real overlapping booking rather than trusting a claim, price the team you have rather than a single seat, and add the processor's own transaction fee before calling any number final.

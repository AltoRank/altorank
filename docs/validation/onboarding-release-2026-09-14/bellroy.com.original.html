How to compare carry-on luggage: hardside vs softside, materials, weight, and durability

Carry-on luggage is any bag sized to fit in an overhead bin or under the seat in front of you, letting you skip checked-bag fees and baggage claim entirely. The two main structural types are hardside (a rigid polycarbonate or aluminum shell) and softside (a flexible nylon-wrapped frame). Choosing between them comes down to three things: how much the bag weighs empty, how it holds up to daily abuse, and what it's made of.

What is carry-on luggage, and what size counts?

There's no single global size rule. Airline limits vary by carrier, which is why luggage brands often design to the most common numbers and tell you to double-check. According to Béis's own sizing chart, American, Delta, United and JetBlue all list 22" H x 14" L x 9" D as their carry-on limit, and Béis built its Hybrid Carry-On Roller to match those exact dimensions. Antler follows a similar logic: most of its carry-on collection is designed within 22 x 14 x 9 in, though its Icon Stripe "Biggest Carry-On" line runs slightly larger, up to 22.8 x 15.5 x 9.4 in, for travelers who want to push the limit. Always confirm the number with your specific airline before you fly, since a bag that clears one carrier's sizer can still get gate-checked on another.

What's the material difference between hardside and softside carry-on luggage?

Hardside carry-ons use a rigid outer shell, almost always polycarbonate, sometimes aluminum on premium lines. Softside carry-ons wrap a frame in woven nylon fabric. Both approaches show up across the same brands, so the material choice is rarely about quality tier, it's about how you want the bag to behave in use.

The practical difference: a hard shell resists punctures and keeps its shape under a stacked baggage cart, while a soft shell flexes around obstacles, often includes exterior pockets a hard shell can't have, and can compress slightly to squeeze into a tight overhead bin.

How much do hardside and softside carry-ons weigh?

Empty weight matters because airlines that enforce carry-on weight limits count the bag itself against your allowance. Béis publishes weights across both material types in the same product line, which makes for a useful side-by-side. Its polycarbonate Carry-On Roller weighs 7.81 lbs, the Small Carry-On Roller weighs 7.15 lbs, and the Hybrid Carry-On Roller (which adds aluminum corner reinforcement) weighs 8.39 lbs. By contrast, the brand's soft-sided, ballistic-nylon Pro Rolling Backpack weighs 6.48 lbs, the lightest bag in that lineup, despite carrying similar capacity.

That pattern, hardshells adding weight for rigidity and reinforcement while nylon softsides trim ounces, is consistent with how Antler frames its own range: Antler describes its hardside carry-ons as "lightweight, practical" while noting its softside cases "often weigh even less." If you're close to an airline's weight cap, checking the manufacturer's published empty weight, not just the liter capacity, is the more reliable comparison.

Which holds up better over years of travel: hardside or softside?

Durability depends less on which material is "tougher" in the abstract and more on what kind of damage you're trying to avoid. Hard shells resist crushing and punctures but can crack at a corner from a hard drop; several brands reinforce that exact weak point. Béis, for example, adds "reinforced aluminum corner protectors and double-coated zipper bumpers" to its Hybrid Carry-On Roller specifically to resist scuffing at impact points. Soft shells don't crack, but their fabric can abrade or tear over repeated abuse, which is why nylon builds lean on denier rating and coatings rather than rigidity.

Away makes a similar durability claim about its own softside line, stating it put the collection through repeated testing so the nylon exterior "remains tear and snag-free, even when facing the most aggressive baggage handling." That's a vendor's own claim about its own product, worth noting as such rather than an independent finding, but it points to what to check on any softside bag: denier weight, coating, and whether the maker backs it with a stated repair or warranty program.

Warranty and repairability are the parts of durability you can actually verify before buying. Bellroy, for instance, covers products for faults in materials and workmanship for up to 10 years from purchase, and states it has "engineered certain luggage parts to be replaced easily by you" using its Fix-it Kits, so a worn wheel or handle doesn't mean replacing the whole bag. Bellroy also builds its bags from recycled materials such as plastic bottles and sources leather from gold-rated Leather Working Group tanneries, which speaks to how the materials are sourced even before durability testing begins. Whichever brand you're comparing, ask the same two questions: how long is the warranty, and can worn parts be repaired or replaced?

A worked example: choosing between hardside and softside

Picture a hypothetical frequent flyer who takes a short domestic work trip roughly twice a month and wants one carry-on that survives two years of gate-checking, cabin overheads, and rideshare trunks. Here's how the comparison plays out using the criteria above, not a specific product recommendation:

  • Weight check: Pull the published empty weight for each shortlisted bag. If two options both claim "carry-on size," the one closer to 6-7 lbs empty (like a nylon softside) leaves more of the airline's total weight allowance for your actual packing.
  • Impact zones: Look at how each bag treats its corners and zippers, the two most common failure points. A hardside with reinforced corners or a softside with padded, coated exterior pockets are both answers to the same problem, just different mechanisms.
  • Repair path: Ask whether the maker sells replacement wheels, handles, or zipper pulls, and check the warranty length. A 10-year warranty with user-replaceable parts changes the long-run cost math versus a bag with no stated repair option.
  • Size fit: Confirm the exact H x L x D against the airlines you fly most, since (as above) the accepted dimensions differ by carrier.

Running a real bag through those four checks, rather than defaulting to "hardside is tougher" or "softside is lighter" as a blanket rule, is what actually predicts how it performs on trip fifty.

Whether you land on hardside, softside, or a hybrid, the comparison always comes back to the same four criteria: what it's made of, how much it weighs empty, how it's built to survive impact, and what happens when a part eventually wears out. Check those four things against your own travel pattern, and the right carry-on luggage for you becomes an easy call rather than a guess.
